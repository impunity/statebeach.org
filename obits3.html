OBITUARIES

O.C. appeals judge Crosby dies
He quoted the Bard and Yogi Berra, and integrity overruled his ambition.

By Larry Welborn
The Orange County Register, Tuesday, January 27, 2004

Justice Thomas Crosby Jr. quoted Shakespeare as easily as he did Yogi Berra.

He also quoted Mark Twain, Dobie Gillis, Lewis Carroll and "Saturday Night Live" characters during 18 years worth of sometimes sarcastic, often funny, and even more often blunt opinions on appeals from Orange County Superior Court.

He earned the nickname "The Quill" because of those clever opinions, which were frequently overturned by a more conservative California Supreme Court.

And when he retired in 2001, he wanted to write.

Crosby, 63, of Tustin was researching a book about an Orange County con man from the 1960s when he died Friday of an apparent heart attack.

He was one of the first four Orange County appellate court judges appointed by then-Gov. Jerry Brown to form a new court in 1982 when legislators broke Orange County cases away from the overworked Los Angeles County appeals bench.

Conservative Orange County politicians lobbied for years for a local appeals court, but they were dismayed when it became a reality in 1982 because Brown had the opportunity to appoint the first four justices. Brown named Crosby and three other Democrats to sit in judgment in Orange County.

Crosby, who wore a bow tie to work every day, quickly became the 4th District Court of Appeal's most colorful justice.

On a particularly heinous murder case in which a businessman persuaded his 14-year-old daughter to kill his wife, Crosby began with the opening lines of Shakespeare's "Hamlet": "Murder most foul, as in the best it is; but this most foul, strange and unnatural."

And then Crosby added, "The Bard of Avon wrote no darker tragedy than this."

He noted in another opinion a line from Shakespeare's "Henry VI": "The first thing we do, let's kill all the lawyers."

The Bard wasn't his only source of inspiration.

"The jury instruction was, as Dobie Gillis might put it, logic proof," he wrote in one opinion.

"Perhaps Yogi Berra said it best, 'You can observe a lot by watching,'" he said in another.

Crosby also could be punny in his opinions. "That dog, as we say out West, won't hunt," he wrote in a dog-bite case. "Westminster is simply barking up the wrong judicial tree."

He once said he was most proud of his decision preventing Santa Ana from enforcing a "no camping" ordinance as a method of controlling the homeless population in the Civic Center area.

The fact that his opinion was reversed by the state Supreme Court didn't detract from his confidence in the correctness of his opinion, he said.

In fact, Crosby unashamedly claimed that he was the most-reversed appellate court justice in California during the years when Republican-appointed Malcolm Lucas was chief justice.

"It always upsets me when our Supreme Court makes those kinds of mistakes," a mischievous Crosby told the Register in 1998.

He wrote the majority opinion that forced the Boy Scouts to admit two Anaheim Hills teenagers who said they didn't believe in God. That decision was reversed.

Crosby attended Stanford University and Boalt Hall Law School at the University of California, Berkeley. He spent two years as a Peace Corps volunteer in Peru before spending five years as a prosecutor in the Orange County.

He later became a top criminal-defense attorney, once getting a reduced verdict for a former Mafia associate who entered the witness-protection program and was subsequently involved in the murder of a drug dealer.

Crosby was named to the Orange County Superior Court bench in 1981 and was elevated to the 4th District appeals court a year later.

Former Justice Ed Wallin said Monday that Crosby could have made a significant contribution to the state Supreme Court had he not spent most of his appellate career during the 16 years when Republican Govs. George Deukmejian and Pete Wilson were making appointments.

Crosby spent most of his tenure in Orange County arguing with Republican appointee David Sills, now the 4th District's presiding judge. Both were accomplished writers who did not hesitate to use the pen to take potshots at the other.

"The (majority) opinion is about as clear on the subject as smoked glass," Crosby once wrote.

The two men respected and admired each other and often played golf together.

"We'd still be arguing cases on the course," Sills said Monday, "probably to the detriment of our golf games."

Crosby is survived by his wife, Patty, and sons, Scott, 34, and Brett, 30, both of San Diego.
